# IBUSHI 燻 — Design source of truth

*Written 2026-07-25, before any component. A builder (not a hotel) in the
Aubade register: a house that raises and revives **minka** — traditional
Japanese villager houses — in Hida, Gifu.*

## The one-line brief

**A minka is cured by the smoke of being lived in.**

The irori's smoke is not a by-product of the house; it is the house's
preservative. Two centuries of it lacquer the beams in soot, drive out
moisture, and kill the insects that would otherwise eat the roof. Stop
lighting the fire and the building begins to die. **Habitation is
structural.** That is the whole argument, and it is the reason this subject
belongs to Aubade — the register of dwelling — rather than to Nocturne.

## Identity

**Name:** **Ibushi** (燻) — to smoke-cure. The verb for what the hearth does
to the frame above it. The word carries a second, older meaning in the idiom
*ibushi-gin* (燻し銀), "smoked silver": something unshowy that reveals its
quality slowly, the opposite of polish. Both readings are the house's
character — the work is dark, slow, and quiet, and it is what keeps the roof
up.

**Series:** **Aubade** — the morning voice, adapted for a *builder* rather
than a host. The native hour is a mountain morning: cold blue air outside,
low sun coming flat through the shoji, and one column of smoke standing off
a thatched ridge. The rooms are warm and inhabited; the light is generous.
This is emphatically not the Nocturne lamp — Kaji and Shokunin already hold
Japan at night, and a minka photographed in chiaroscuro would be a lie about
how these houses are actually used.

**Place:** **Hida (飛騨)**, northern Gifu Prefecture — a valley system of
timber and heavy snow. The choice is load-bearing: from one workshop in Hida
you can reach all four typologies in this site honestly.

- **Nōka** — the farm valleys of the Hida basin.
- **Gasshō-zukuri** — Shirakawa-gō and Gokayama, over the pass.
- **Machiya** — the Sanmachi merchant streets of Takayama, a real preserved
  townhouse quarter.
- **Gyoka** — the Sea of Japan coast at Wajima and the Noto shore, the
  workshop's coastal work.

Hida is also the home of the **Hida no Takumi** (飛騨の匠) — the carpenters
who, from the Nara period, paid the province's tax in *labour* instead of
rice: ten men a year sent to build the capitals. The lineage is real, it is
documented, and it is the only claim of pedigree this site makes.

**The workshop:** eleven people. **Ōno Sōhachi** (大野宗八), *tōryō* (棟梁,
master carpenter), sixth of his line to hold the title. A thatcher, **Mizuno
Kaneko**, who manages the workshop's own *kayaba*. Six carpenters, two
apprentices, one plasterer.

**Proposition:** abandoned minka — *akiya* — brought back into use in the
four traditions of one valley, and kept alive by their fires. Ibushi is
asked about forty houses a year and takes four.

## The akiya reposition (2026-07-26)

The thesis always implied an antagonist and never named it. The site already
said *"an empty one is a structural emergency and not merely a sad thing"* and
closed on *"will the fire be lit?"* — that is an akiya business, argued but
not declared. Naming it costs nothing and buys the stakes.

**The condition, with real figures** (all published, all cited on the page):

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| 9,000,000 vacant dwellings · 13.8% of the housing stock, double 1993 | 2023 Housing and Land Survey (MIC), published April 2024 |
| ~60% inherited · >70% of those pre-1980 · >70% showing decay · ~60% vacated on the owner's death | MLIT, 2024 Survey on Akiya House Owners |
| Kominka fell **13%** between 2008 and 2013 | MIC survey |
| ~1,250,000 kominka thought to remain | Japan Kominka Association estimate |

**The kominka figure is the one that belongs to Ibushi.** Nine million is a
national statistic about empty flats in Osaka; −13% in five years is the
specific building type this workshop works on, disappearing. Lead with the
nine million for scale, land on the 13% for stakes.

**Two rules that keep this from becoming a trend explainer:**

1. **The fire stays primary.** Akiya is the *condition Ibushi works in*, not
   the brand. The one-line brief does not change and the H1 does not change.
   If a visitor leaves knowing the vacancy rate but not why smoke is
   structural, the page has failed.
2. **Never the "¥500 Japanese house" genre.** That content is everywhere and
   is the exact inverse of this workshop. Ibushi does not sell a cheap house;
   it tells you most of them cannot be saved.

**Selectivity had to become a standard, not a claim.** "We are selective"
reads as taste, which would be fatal here. `/mitate` publishes the whole rule
— frame pre-war and hand-cut, daikokubashira sound to the core, roof
re-thatchable rather than merely replaceable, and somebody who will light the
fire — each written so it can fail, with what it fails on. Declining is then
professional judgement rather than a pose, which is also the honest reading of
the data: most akiya genuinely are post-1980 stock with nothing worth a
carpenter's year.

**The appraisal is the site's under-¥750,000 product** (order-form doctrine):
a fixed-fee day on site and a written verdict, ¥180,000 in Hida, credited in
full against the work. Fixed rather than a percentage, because a percentage
gives the workshop a reason to say yes.

## The register argument (recorded because it was the call)

- **Native hour.** A minka lives at **breakfast** — the fire lit, smoke
  standing off the ridge into cold air, low sun through paper. Warm interior
  daylight is the ground. Aubade.
- **Not a hotel.** Aubade's opening subjects host; Ibushi *builds*. So the
  doctrine bends the way Veta bent it: hospitality becomes the evidence of
  habitation — a kettle on the jizaikagi, a floor swept, sandals at the
  kamachi, a roof being re-thatched by neighbours. Never a guest, never a
  model in a yukata.
- **Distinct from every Japanese sibling.** Kaji (knives) and Shokunin
  (armour) are Nocturnes about *objects under one lamp*. Ibushi is about
  *architecture in daylight*, and shares neither their palette, their type,
  nor their hour.
- **Distinct from the timber siblings.** Tyri and Malmfuru build Norwegian
  log houses — solid horizontal logs, cold snow-light or midwinter dark.
  Ibushi is a post-and-beam frame under thatch, in warm interior daylight,
  in another hemisphere of craft. Different material system, different
  register, different argument.

**The okibi rule** (this site's version of the metal accent): ember appears
only where a fire actually is — the live stroke in the ink drawing as it
redraws, the irori mark on a plan, focus rings, one small state indicator.
**Primary buttons are susu, not ember.** The Aubade anti-pattern list names
"cream + Didone + terracotta CTA by reflex" as an instant fail; an ember CTA
would land exactly there no matter how well justified. If okibi exceeds ~3%
of a frame, it has stopped being a fire and become paint.

Body text is susu on washi (≈15.8:1). Tan-on-cream is banned outright.

## Type

Kaji already owns **Shippori Mincho + Newsreader**; Shokunin owns **Zen Old
Mincho**. Neither may reappear. The pairing here is chosen for a *warm,
domestic, daylit* voice rather than a formal one, and covers both scripts
properly:

- **Faustina** — the Latin display and editorial voice. A humanist old-style
  with low stroke contrast and sturdy, slightly soft serifs. Its proportions
  sit unusually well beside Japanese type because it is not a high-contrast
  Didone; it carries statements, chapter openings, and long editorial
  passages without becoming "luxury magazine."
- **Zen Antique** — the Japanese display voice. A warm, archaic mincho with
  a woodblock-printed weight to it. Used for the terms themselves at
  display size (囲炉裏, 土間, 合掌造り) — never as decoration behind Latin
  text.
- **IBM Plex Sans JP** — the working voice, in both scripts: dimensions in
  ken and shaku, roof pitch in sun, tsubo counts, tatami counts, form
  labels, navigation, captions. Quiet, technical, unfussy.

No sibling uses Faustina, Zen Antique, or IBM Plex Sans JP. Avoid
uppercase-everything, extreme-contrast Didones, and low-contrast body text.

## Signature geometry

**The snapped line.** A carpenter marks timber with a *sumitsubo* — an ink
pot with a chalked cord that is plucked to snap a dead-straight line onto a
curved, irregular log. Every rule, divider, and underline on this site is
that line: 1px susu, dead straight, full-bleed where it can be. The only
curve permitted anywhere in the chrome is the **magari-bari** — the natural
bent pine beam — which appears once, as the section divider before the
frame chapter. Straight lines snapped onto crooked timber is the entire
visual thesis of Japanese carpentry, and it is enough ornament.

## The signature interaction — **Sumitsuke** 墨付け: the marking

*Sumitsuke* is the real term for the master carpenter marking every timber
with the ink line before a single cut is made. Nothing is cut until the line
is drawn. That is what this page does.

A large orthographic drawing — **section and plan together** — rendered in
susu ink strokes on washi, redrawing live from the client's own inputs:

1. **Typology** (nōka / gyoka / machiya / gasshō) — the master control.
   Changes the whole silhouette at once: roof pitch, storey count, footprint
   aspect, cladding, and which rooms exist. A gasshō rears up to 60° with
   three attic floors; a machiya flattens to a 4-sun tile pitch and stretches
   backwards into an eel's bed.
2. **Bays** — width in ken × depth in ken, on the real module. The plan grows
   in discrete bays, the tatami grid fills in behind it, and the section's
   span widens with the beam it would actually need.
3. **Site exposure** (inland / heavy snow / coastal) — steepens the kōbai and
   thickens the thatch under snow load; switches the cladding to *yakisugi*
   charred cedar and adds roof weighting on the coast. A real constraint
   changing a real dimension.
4. **Doma ratio** — how much of the ground plane stays earth floor versus
   raised yuka. Drags the agarikamachi line across the plan; the irori sits
   in the yuka and moves with it.
5. **Ken module** (inakama 6.0 shaku / kyōma 6.5 shaku) — the regional
   variant. Rescales everything, quietly, the way it actually would.

All five feed **one parametric model** driving one SVG, so section, plan,
tatami grid, and hearth move together in a single redraw — not five widgets.
The active stroke flashes **okibi** for one beat as it redraws, the way wet
ink sits on timber before it dries.

A live **spec block** states, in the craft's own units: total *tsubo*, tatami
count in *jō*, ridge height in *shaku*, *kōbai* in sun (with the degree in
parentheses), roof area in m², thatch mass in bundles, and the frame's
principal span. **"Snap this line"** carries the entire configuration into
`/commission` pre-filled.

**JS off / `?nofx` / reduced motion:** the SVG renders a sensible default
(nōka, 4×6 ken, inland, inakama) fully drawn with the spec printed, and all
five control groups present as real radios and ranges — the form still
submits the chosen spec. No animation, never a broken frame.

### Why not time-of-day

The Aubade profile now bans light-over-time by default, and it is right to:
Lagar do Sol, Aresta, Dar el Warda, and Larès have spent it. This signature
is **spatial/structural and configurable** — the two axes the collection is
thinnest on — and it is inevitable for the subject in a way a sun-line never
would be, because a Japanese house genuinely *is* a modular grid before it
is anything else.

## The second interaction — the irori seats

Small, on `/rooms`, and not competing with the signature. The four sides of a
hearth are named and ranked, and every minka in Japan obeyed it:

- **Yokoza** 横座 — the head of the household, back to the wall, facing the
  doma. No one else sits here, ever.
- **Kakaza** 嬶座 — the wife, on the kitchen side, within reach of the pots.
- **Kyakuza** 客座 — the guest, opposite the kakaza.
- **Kijiri** 木尻 — literally "wood's end," where the firewood is fed in.
  Children, apprentices, and the lowest-ranking sit here, in the smoke.

An SVG plan of a hearth; focus or hover a side and it names who sat there and
why. Keyboard-navigable, fully legible with JS off (all four labels printed).
It proves the thesis better than any adjective: the fire organised the family.

## How the site takes an order

**Both paths, per commitment 7.**

- **A house is a commission.** A raised or revived minka runs from roughly
  ¥18,000,000 for a small nōka revival into the hundreds of millions for a
  full gasshō relocation — far above the $5,000 line. `/commission` is a
  proper structured brief, not a "write to us" gesture: the site, the
  typology, the bays, the exposure, the intended use, the timeline, the
  budget band — pre-filled from Sumitsuke when the client arrives from there.
- **The bench also sells small work**, and that genuinely sits under $5,000:
  *susutake* — soot-blackened bamboo pulled from a roof at re-thatch and
  sold by the pole; a *jizaikagi* with a carved fish counterweight; a
  *chōna*-finished shelf board; a bundle of *kaya* thatch. These get a real
  order form on `/commission`, with quantities and prices in yen.

Both hand off by **mailto** with `enctype="text/plain"` so every field lands
legibly. Never a card, never a checkout. Both work with JS off.

## Copy voice

Declarative, dimensional, and unhurried. Numbers in the craft's units. "A
four-by-six-ken nōka, 24 tsubo, 17-sun kōbai, roof re-thatched in 2019 by
forty people in one day." Say what was done, when, by whom, and in what
material. No "timeless elegance," no "the soul of Japan," no wabi-sabi as an
adjective, no Zen. The word *zen* does not appear on this site. If a
sentence could be written by someone who had never stood in a minka, cut it.

Japanese terms are given in rōmaji, then kanji, then a plain-English gloss on
first use — and never used as decoration once glossed.

## Imagery — the Ibushi recipe

**One recipe: cold mountain morning outside, warm inhabited daylight inside.**
Generous light, fully modelling every subject. Interiors are lit by low sun
raking through shoji paper plus the hearth's own glow — bright and legible,
never murky. Target mean luma 90–150 for exteriors, 70–120 for interiors.
Chiaroscuro is Kaji's lamp and is banned here.

**Constant batch phrase** (identical across every prompt):
*"cold clear mountain morning light, low sun raking through translucent paper
screens, warm soot-blackened timber and pale straw and earth-plaster tones,
documentary architectural photography, natural irregularity, no text, no
signage, no lettering."*

**Human presence is required** (doctrine, and this subject needs it —
inhabitation is literally the thesis): the tōryō at the timber, the thatcher
on a roof, a kettle being hung on the jizaikagi, a village re-thatching a
gasshō roof under the yui system.

`hero-smoke` is **not** retired by the reposition — it is promoted. It now
runs full-bleed lower on the home page as *the other end of the work*: the
same kind of building, smoking at seven in the morning. The page opens on a
cold house and closes the section on a lit one, which is the whole argument
in two frames.

Note the copy fix that came with it: the hero line read *"There is no
chimney"* directly above a plate showing a ridge vent with smoke coming out
of it. Minka have no **flue from the hearth** — the smoke passes up through
the open frame — but ridge smoke-vents are real and common. The copy now says
"no flue", which is both true and no longer contradicted by the picture.

Avoid: cherry blossom, red torii, geisha, Mount Fuji, koi ponds, "zen
garden" raked gravel, calligraphy as wallpaper, generated Japanese text of
any kind. This is a working building trade in a cold valley, not a postcard.
