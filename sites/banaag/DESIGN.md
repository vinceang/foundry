# BANAAG — design source of truth

*Written 2026-08-09, before any component. A custom jeepney body shop in San
Fernando, Pampanga, in the third register: **Vesper**.*

## The one-line brief

**We build the body. The man with the rag finishes it, every morning, before
the sun.**

A jeepney leaves this shop unfinished, and everybody here knows it. What
completes it happens at 04:41 on a concrete apron somewhere in Luzon: the cock
goes off, the operator carries his coffee out to the unit he has been paying
off for six years, finds one spot of dust on a panel he polished last night,
wipes it, and stands there for a minute and a half looking at the thing before
the day starts taking from him. That minute is the product. The shop builds the
excuse for it.

This is why the site is not a vehicle catalogue. It is a shop that understands
exactly what it is selling.

## The anti-luxury clause (read this before writing a single line of copy)

**This is the first Foundry site whose buyer is not rich, and that is the
point.**

Twenty-two of the twenty-three sites in this collection sell scarcity to
people who have money: a $30,000 violin, a suite on the Costa Vicentina, a
hood for a falcon, an introduction to a charter fleet. Their tone is
*restraint as privilege* — few made, by appointment, write to us.

A jeepney operator is not that buyer. He is a working man who has one or two
units, pays a driver's *boundary* against fuel and a franchise, and finances a
new body over three to five years because it is the largest purchase of his
life after his house. Pricing it like a luxury good would be a lie, and
gatekeeping it would be an insult.

So Banaag is **bespoke without luxury**, and every design decision follows:

- **The filter is passion, not money.** The shop does turn work away — but on
  whether the operator has a picture in his head and will sit at the chalk for
  it, never on his bank. The stated standard is: *"Come with something you
  actually want. We will build that. If you want the same body as everyone
  else, there are faster shops and we will tell you which."* That is the
  discriminating audience test met honestly.
- **Prices are published, in pesos, in full, with terms.** For this buyer the
  number is the anxiety, and hiding it behind an enquiry form would be the
  cruellest possible version of the "write to us" mannerism. Every figure on
  this site is visible, itemised, and adds up. Instalment terms (*hulog*) and
  old-body trade-in are stated plainly, because they are how this is actually
  bought.
- **The voice is welcoming and direct.** No "one may enquire." No
  by-appointment fog. The shop is on the highway, the gate is open, there is
  coffee, and Merced will chalk it out with you on a Tuesday. Short sentences,
  real numbers, second person, warm.
- **Restraint on the page is not scarcity — it is deference.** The site is
  austere because the vehicle is loud, not because emptiness signals expense.
  Same visual discipline as its siblings, opposite reason for it.
- **Dignity is non-negotiable.** The operator is a small businessman with a
  capital asset, photographed as one. Never a picturesque poor man, never
  "colourful chaos," never a foreigner's window seat.

## Identity

**Name:** **Banaag** — Tagalog for the first faint gleam of light before
sunrise, and, in the same word, a gleam or reflection thrown off a surface. One
word that holds both the hour the site is set in and the material the shop
works: polished stainless. A body shop named for what light does when it lands
on steel.

**Place:** **Barangay Sindalan, City of San Fernando, Pampanga** — on the
MacArthur Highway, in the metalworking belt of Central Luzon. Not Las Piñas
(where the famous names are), not Laguna. San Fernando is chosen for a real
lineage, below.

Coordinates for the map: **15.0233 N, 120.6586 E**.

**The shop:** twenty-two men, one buck, one paint bay, one letterer. Between
nine and fourteen bodies a year. Established 1978 by Ambrosio "Boyet" Dizon, a
parol wireman who took a welding job in the dry season and never went back to
lanterns full-time. Run now by his daughter **Merced Dizon-Lansang**, who
signs the plate on every body that leaves.

**Proposition:** a jeepney body fabricated from a bare chassis to one
operator's route, his capacity, his saint, and his colour of light.

### The lineage (this is the site's provenance argument)

San Fernando is the **Christmas capital of the Philippines**, and has run the
*Ligligan Parul* — the Giant Lantern Festival — since 1908. Its lanterns are
five metres across, steel-framed, and carry thousands of bulbs whose flashing
is sequenced by a hand-built drum called a **rotor**: a rotating cylinder wound
with foil contacts that a wireman cuts and files by hand until the light runs
around the lantern in the pattern he heard in his head.

That is the same trade as a jeepney's light valance, done by the same families,
in the same barangays, in the other half of the year. Every parol maker in San
Fernando can wire a jeepney; several of them do. The site's sixth chapter is
this, and it is why the shop's electrician is the most senior man on the floor
after the panel beater.

## The register argument (recorded because it was the call)

**Series: Vesper**, and the backlog was right — but the reason needs stating,
because half of Vesper's subject rule fails here.

Vesper's rule is *things in transit; worlds built on not being seen*. A jeepney
is the purest ground-level case of the first half and a flat contradiction of
the second. It is the most-seen vehicle in the country. It is decorated for
exactly one purpose: so that a commuter standing at a corner in the dark picks
**this** unit and not the one behind it. Nothing in the collection is less
discreet.

The register still holds, because **the register is a decision about light**,
and this subject's hour is not in question. The Philippine dispatch hour is
04:30 to 05:20 — civil twilight, sky going from indigo to a gold band over the
rice, the shop's tube lights still on because the sun has not arrived to make
them pointless. That is the Vesper band exactly.

What changes — and this is what makes it a different Vesper from the three
that exist — is **where the light comes from**. Auriga and Velum take it from
the horizon; Hurr takes it from a lantern at arm's length. Here **the subject
is itself the light source**: the valance tubes are on, the stainless is
throwing the sky back at you, and the chrome is doing at 04:41 exactly what it
was bolted on to do. The afterglow is behind the vehicle. The vehicle is lit
from its own fittings.

This inversion is written into `docs/foundry-series.md` rather than smuggled.

**Distance from Nipis** (the collection's other Philippine site, and the reason
the backlog flagged it): Nipis is Aubade, Laguna, textile, quiet, two hands,
backlit morning, a garment held to a window. Banaag is Vesper, Pampanga, steel,
loud, twenty-two men, pre-dawn, a vehicle on an apron. Provinces do not touch;
registers do not touch; the only shared thing is a country, and the country is
large.

**The two-light rule** (Vesper's second commitment, held strictly):

- **Liwayway is the afterglow.** Structure only — dimension lines, chapter
  facts, focus rings, one button per view. It is the sky, so it never fills a
  panel.
- **Tubo is the instrument light**, and its role is literal: it appears *only*
  where the jeepney's own electricity is on — the valance line in the build
  drawing, the lit signboard, the rotor diagram, the shop's open indicator.
  If Tubo shows up anywhere the vehicle is not powered, it has become
  decoration; delete it.

Two accents, one warm and one cool, is not the register's forbidden "second
warm accent." Both warm would be.

**The counter-pressure rule.** A jeepney is a maximalist object. The site is
therefore austere: teal-black, steel, one gold, one aqua, and enormous amounts
of empty apron. **All saturated colour in this site lives inside the
photographs and inside the buyer's own build drawing.** Chrome, enamel red,
cobalt, the mural — those are the vehicle's, not the page's. The page is the
dark yard the vehicle is standing in. This is the whole art direction and it is
what lets the details read.

## Type

- **Big Shoulders Display (400/600/700)** — the display voice. A condensed
  industrial signage face drawn from civic lettering: tall, narrow, built for
  a board that has to be read across four lanes. It is what a *letrista*'s
  block hand becomes when it is drawn rather than brushed, and it is the face
  the buyer's own route name is set in on the drawn signboard — so the display
  voice and the signature interaction are the same decision. Unused in the
  collection.
- **Public Sans (400/500/600, tabular figures)** — the working voice. A civic
  utility grotesque with the exact temperature of a franchise application. A
  jeepney is a *registered* object — plate, body number, franchise, route,
  seating capacity — and the working voice should read like the paperwork that
  makes it legal. Numbers everywhere: millimetres, gauge, seats, pesos.
  Unused in the collection.

Deliberately not: Instrument Serif (Auriga), Spectral + IBM Plex Mono (Velum),
Amiri + Barlow (Hurr). No serif at all — this is the collection's first site
with none, and a coachbuilder's drawing has no business in a book face.
No third mono in the register.

## The opening move

**The chalk dimension in the margin.**

Chapters open **cold** — the display statement, nothing above it. Running down
the left margin rail beside it is one measured fact about the thing in view,
set in the working voice between two end ticks, the way a body layout is
annotated on the shop floor:

```
   ├── 5 940 mm ──┤        (the body length under discussion)
   ├── 1,2 mm ────┤        (the gauge of the panel being beaten)
   ├── 18 upuan ──┤        (the capacity the chapter is about)
   ├── 04:41 ─────┤        (the hour)
```

It carries real information — deleting it loses a fact — and it is the
drawing's own convention rather than a graphic one. Held site-wide.

Explicitly **not** a tracked caps label above a heading (Nocturne's spent
move), and deliberately **not** Hurr's inline dot-separated readout, which is
the same idea in a form the register has now used.

**Signature geometry: the dimension line.** The site's only ornament is a
hairline with a perpendicular tick at each end — the measurement callout from a
coachbuilder's elevation. Section rules are dimension lines. The build
drawing's annotations are dimension lines. Not arcs (Auriga), not flight rails
(Velum), not saddle stitch (Hurr). Zero border-radius anywhere except where the
vehicle itself is round.

## Signature interaction — "Ang hulma" (the buck)

The one bold spend, built and critiqued first, and fused with the order per
doctrine.

A jeepney body is not bought off a lot. It is specified — chassis, capacity,
roof, fittings, lettering — and then chalked out full size on the shop floor
before a single panel is cut. **So the configurator is the shop's actual first
working document**, and the visitor is standing at the chalk.

**The visitor specifies a body, and a side elevation redraws live:**

| Input | What it changes in the drawing |
|---|---|
| **Chassis** (Isuzu 4BE1 / 4HF1 / Mitsubishi 4D34 / surplus) | Nose length, cab height, wheel diameter, and the price floor |
| **Kapasidad** — 14 / 16 / 18 / 20 / 22 *upuan* | The **wheelbase stretches and the window bays multiply** — four bays at 14, eight at 22. The vehicle changes proportion, not scale |
| **Bubong** (roof) — flat / crowned / with *kargahan* rack | The roofline and the rack rails redraw |
| **Kabayo** — none / single stallion / pair / stallion and bumper sculpture | The chrome on the hood and the front |
| **Ilaw** — the valance tube colour | The tube line under the roof lights in the chosen colour, and **the whole drawing picks up that colour on its reflective surfaces** |
| **Karatula** — the buyer types their route | Their own text appears **hand-lettered on the drawn signboard**, in the site's display face |
| **Dedikasyon** — the buyer types a dedication | Lettered along the *estribo*, where a jeepney carries its owner's line |

The two typed fields are the point. Every jeepney in the Philippines carries a
route board and a dedication — a hometown, a patron saint, a wife, a decade in
Riyadh — and typing yours and watching it appear on the body is the moment the
vehicle becomes *your* vehicle rather than a configuration. Nothing in the
collection has put the buyer's own words on the product.

**Then the same panel is the build sheet.** It totals in pesos off the real
spec, it has two modes — **Buong katawan** (a whole body) and **Kabit lang**
(fittings onto a unit you already run) — and it files the order. Choosing and
commissioning are one act.

Nothing is templated from Veta: that draws a surfboard *outline* from a rider's
numbers. This one multiplies structural bays, and then lets a stranger write on
the side of it.

## Page arc

**One page thesis:** *a body is finished by the man who wipes it, so build it
for him.*

I. **04:41** — the hero: the operator, the coffee, the rag, the unit with its
   tubes still on. Vince's minute, and the site's argument.
II. **Ang hulmahan** — the shop: twenty-two men, one buck, nine to fourteen
   bodies a year, and what "a body" actually means on a bare chassis.
III. **Ang hulma** — **the signature**: chalk out your body, and file it.
IV. **Ang detalye** — the fittings, plate by plate: *kabayo*, *karatula*,
   *estribo*, *salamin*, *tubo*, *sabit*. Each plate opens into its record —
   gauge, dimension, who makes it, what it costs. **This is where the
   jeepney's details are made to come out.**
V. **Ang letrista** — Nolan Simbulan, twenty-nine years on the brush, and the
   four hands he letters in. Human presence.
VI. **Ang rotor** — the parol lineage: why the electrician outranks everyone.
VII. **Inilabas** — the archive of bodies that have left, by operator, route,
   capacity and year.
VIII. **Colophon** — the plate, signed.

## How the site takes an order

Two real price bands, so the site does both things commitment 7 allows —
and, per the anti-luxury clause, **every number is on the page**:

- **A whole body:** ₱780,000 – ₱1,450,000 (roughly $13,500 – $25,000). Above
  the $5,000 line, but it still gets a **structured build sheet**, not a
  precious "write to us" letter — because a body shop takes a spec, and the
  spec is the document. The configurator *is* that form, and it itemises: the
  buyer sees what each decision costs while making it.
- **Fittings only:** ₱2,400 – ₱52,000 (roughly $42 – $900). Well under the
  line, so this is a plain, honest **order form** — the `Kabit lang` mode of
  the same sheet, with line items and a total.

**Terms are part of the product and appear beside the total:**

- 30% *paunang bayad* (deposit) to enter the queue, the balance in *hulog*
  over 24, 36 or 48 months, stated as an actual monthly figure the buyer can
  compare against a day's takings.
- Old body accepted against the price, assessed on the floor.
- The queue is honest and public: nine to fourteen bodies a year, so the sheet
  says which month you would be starting in.

Fictional brand: the sheet collects the order and hands off an enquiry.
**Never a card number.** Every state designed — rest, focus, filled, error,
submitting, filed — in the register's voice, with the confirmation written as a
**shop receipt** (what was filed, your place in the queue, and that Merced
calls you, not the other way round).

## Imagery

Constant art-direction phrase across the whole batch:

> *photographed at 04:40 in Pampanga, twenty minutes before sunrise; deep
> teal-black tropical sky overhead with a low gold band on the horizon; the
> vehicle lit by its own chrome, stainless and coloured fluorescent valance
> tubes; generously lit and fully modelled, never murky; large-format, fine
> grain, no text*

Shot list:

1. **Hero / human presence** — the operator on a wet concrete apron at 04:41,
   coffee in one hand, rag in the other, standing back from his jeepney; the
   valance tubes on; gold band low behind the rice.
2. The stainless flank close — brushed grain, rivets, the chrome strip, the
   sky in the reflection.
3. **Human presence** — the *letrista* at the panel, brush loaded, one line of
   lettering half-finished (no legible words — lettering is drawn in the site,
   never generated).
4. The *kabayo* — chrome stallion on the hood, hard raking light.
5. The light valance lit — coloured tubes under the roof edge, interior
   mirrored panels catching them.
6. The buck and the bare chassis in the shop, tube lights on, dawn in the
   doorway.
7. The parol rotor — a hand-wound contact drum, wire, foil.
8. Fittings on a bench — *estribo*, grab rail, mirror kit, laid out flat.

**No text in images, ever** — every piece of lettering on this site is drawn
in SVG or set in type, which is also why the display face had to be a signage
face.

## Never

- Not Nocturne's chiaroscuro. A page darker than a tropical dawn is the
  register's canonical failure and has been rejected once already.
- Not a fourth apricot afterglow. Liwayway is a true gold, and Tubo is the
  register's first cool accent.
- Not a tracked caps label above a heading.
- Not a light-over-time signature. The collection is crowded with those; this
  subject's axis is *structure multiplying to a capacity*.
- Not poverty-tourism, and not kitsch-tourism either. No "colourful chaos of
  Manila," no smoke, no crowds shot from a car window. This is a manufacturer
  with twenty-two employees and a queue.
- Not Nipis's world. Loud, steel, male, provincial-industrial. Different
  register, different province, different century of the same country.
